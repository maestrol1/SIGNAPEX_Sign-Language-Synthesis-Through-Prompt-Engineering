How to read the recognition scores

Every video here carries two kinds of score: a machine reading (from MediaPipe and SigLIP2) and a human reading. They don't always agree — and where they disagree, the human is usually closer to the truth.

The machine ticks can be misleading

A green check from MediaPipe or SigLIP2 means the model's best guess matched the target letter. It does not mean the hand in the video actually forms that letter clearly. These models were built to label photos of real, correct handshapes, so when they meet a blurry, melting, or malformed AI-generated hand they still pick some letter — and sometimes that guess lands on the right one by luck. A “correct” machine score can sit on top of a video that no person would read as that letter.

Why we kept the machine scores anyway

They're still useful as a rough, repeatable signal across all 500 videos — no human could stay perfectly consistent over that many clips. But they work best as a screen, not a verdict. Treat them as “worth a closer look,” then let the video and the human rating decide.

What the human scores add

The human rating (Track 2) records what a real viewer actually saw: which letter they read, how close the handshape looked, and overall sign quality. This is the part that answers the real research question — can a person recognise the sign? — which is what fingerspelling is ultimately for.

Rule of thumb: when the green tick and your own eyes disagree, believe your eyes. Open the video, read the prompt, and check the human rating before drawing any conclusion about a single clip.

One more caveat for the statistics: the human ratings come from a single rater, so very small differences between strategies or generators sit within normal rating noise and shouldn't be over-read. The large gaps (for example, how much better straight-finger letters fare than curved ones) are the trustworthy part.
